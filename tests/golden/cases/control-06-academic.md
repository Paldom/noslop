Archival records of municipal water pricing offer an unusually clean window
into nineteenth-century infrastructure politics. Between 1847 and 1893, the
city council revised tariffs eleven times. Each revision followed a drought
or an epidemic, never an election. This timing suggests that public health
crises, rather than electoral pressure, drove pricing reform in this period.

The ledgers themselves complicate the picture. Collection rates hovered near
60 percent for decades, and enforcement records show that arrears were
pursued selectively. Wealthy districts accumulated larger debts yet faced
fewer disconnections. Whether this reflects administrative capacity or
deliberate policy is difficult to establish from the ledgers alone; council
minutes are silent on enforcement criteria.

We read this silence cautiously. Administrative records document decisions,
not intentions, and the absence of stated criteria may indicate either
discretion or indifference. What the ledgers do establish is the pattern:
crisis-driven reform, uneven enforcement, and a persistent gap between
tariff policy and collected revenue that no revision closed.
