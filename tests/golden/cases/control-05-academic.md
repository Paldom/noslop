The relationship between task-switching frequency and perceived productivity
remains contested in the literature. Prior survey work suggests that
knowledge workers switch tasks every 10 to 12 minutes on average, although
estimates vary considerably with measurement method. Diary studies report
lower frequencies than passive logging, which may reflect recall bias rather
than behavioral difference.

Our sample consisted of 214 participants across four organizations. We
logged application focus events for 15 working days and administered weekly
self-report instruments. Participants whose switching frequency fell in the
top quartile reported lower perceived productivity, though the effect was
modest and attenuated after controlling for meeting load. We caution against
causal interpretation; switching may respond to workload rather than drive
it.

Several limitations apply. Focus events approximate attention imperfectly,
and our organizations skew toward software development. The instrument we
used has known ceiling effects. Nonetheless, the association was consistent
across sites, and the direction agrees with earlier experimental work on
resumption costs. Replication with experience sampling would strengthen the
evidence considerably.
